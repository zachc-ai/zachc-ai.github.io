I scribbled three lines in my notebook this morning. They came out fast, the way things do when you've been chewing on them for months without realizing it.

> Don't chase hype blindly.
> Don't ignore trends willfully.
> Hold your ground, embrace change, and cherish everything you have and everyone you meet.

I work in AI infrastructure. Every morning I open my laptop to a new "revolution": vibe coding, another agent framework, another startup promising to rewrite everything. Last week's trending repo is already yesterday's news.

Yet something real *is* shifting underneath all that noise. GPU compute is rewriting the entire stack, and the infrastructure work I do every day barely existed three years ago. Ignoring real change is just as dangerous as chasing fake change.

There's no clean formula for separating signal from noise, but three anchors keep bringing me back to earth.

## I. Don't chase hype

In January 2019, WeWork was valued at $47 billion. Nine months later it couldn't even IPO, its valuation fell 78%, the CEO walked out with $700 million, and by 2023 it was bankrupt. In May 2022, Terra/Luna wiped out $45 billion in a week, and FTX, Celsius, and Three Arrows Capital fell right behind it. Most people rushing in didn't understand the tech; they just watched everyone else make money.

The script barely changes. A real innovation (shared workspaces, blockchains) gets inflated by capital and FOMO until the price tag floats free of reality, and the followers absorb the crash.

Buffett's line on the "circle of competence" still says it best:

> "The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital."

Chasing hype means stepping outside your circle while convincing yourself you're still inside. Naval puts the same point in five words: "Escape competition through authenticity." Staying out of a stampede still takes discipline. You have to ask yourself honestly: *do I actually understand this, or am I just scared of missing out?*

AI is running the exact same cycle. YC's Jared Friedman noted that a quarter of the Winter 2025 batch had codebases that were 95% AI-generated. Sounds impressive until you look at the other side of the ledger. When CodeRabbit audited 470 open-source pull requests, AI-co-authored ones carried 1.57× the security findings of human-only ones (2.74× for XSS). In METR's early-2025 randomized trial, 16 experienced open-source developers working on repos they already knew well actually took 19% longer with AI tools. Even Andrej Karpathy's original definition of "vibe coding" was to "forget that the code even exists," which reads more like a warning label than a best practice. Meanwhile, the SEC has started fining companies for "AI washing," slapping "AI-powered" on products with nothing meaningful underneath. It's the `.com` suffix of our decade.

Laozi said the highest good is like water: it benefits all things and doesn't compete. Water doesn't rush toward the crowd; it settles into the low places nobody wants. Not competing doesn't mean standing still. It just means refusing to get dragged along by somebody else's current.

## II. Don't ignore trends

In 1975, Kodak engineer Steven Sasson built the world's first digital camera at 10,000 pixels. Management looked at it, looked at their billion-dollar film business, and told him to shelve it. Kodak filed for bankruptcy in 2012. They didn't miss the technology; they invented it, saw where it led, and looked away.

Blockbuster managed an even cleaner own-goal. In 2000, Netflix offered to sell itself to them for $50 million. Blockbuster, sitting on nearly 7,700 stores and about 96,000 employees, laughed them out of the room. A decade later Blockbuster was bankrupt and Netflix was on its way to hundreds of billions. A former Blockbuster executive later summed it up: digital would have changed their business, but Blockbuster killed itself.

Marcus Aurelius wasn't writing self-help when he jotted down in his camp notebook that "the universe is change; our life is what our thoughts make it," and urged himself to remember how much nature loves altering whatever already exists. Laozi used water for the same point: nothing is softer, yet nothing wears down hard rock better. Water doesn't turn into a rock to fight a rock; it flows around, seeps in, and eventually splits it open.

Trends behave like water. You don't have to love them, and you can't pretend they aren't there. GPU compute reshaping the computing stack is my Monday morning reality, not hype. LLMs rewriting software engineering's old assumptions are the same.

The difference between hype and a trend is simple: when the easy money pulls out, is it still there?

## III. Hold your ground, embrace change, cherish everything

If dodging hype and respecting trends were easy, everyone would be doing both. The real trick is the third line: holding your ground and embracing change at the same time.

NVIDIA is the clearest case study I know. In 1993, three founders started it at a Denny's with $200 each. By 1997 it was a month from running out of cash, and Jensen Huang's recurring line in meetings was "We are thirty days from going out of business." He later said that if he'd known it would be "a million times harder," he might never have started.

In 2006, NVIDIA shipped CUDA for general-purpose GPU computing. Almost nobody cared: not game studios, not scientific computing, not Wall Street. Huang tried mobile chips when phones heated up and backed out when Tegra didn't stick. He didn't pivot to social media, and he didn't rebrand as a crypto company when miners cleared out every GPU on earth. He stayed parked on GPU compute for thirty years. When the AI wave finally broke, NVIDIA was the only one holding the full infrastructure, and in October 2025 it became the first company to cross $5 trillion in market cap.

Chinese distills a line from Sun Tzu's *Art of War* into four characters: 守正出奇, hold the orthodox foundation and strike with the extraordinary. Hold your ground so your base doesn't drift; embrace change so you move fast on top of it. NVIDIA's foundation was GPU compute, and its edge was already being ready every time a new wave arrived.

Zeng Guofan put the price of entry bluntly: shouting opinions from the sidelines accomplishes nothing; you have to step into the arena and shoulder the weight before success is even possible.

There's also a Buddhist image I keep coming back to, from verse 401 of the Dhammapada: water resting on a lotus leaf, or a mustard seed balanced on the tip of a needle. The lotus leaf doesn't push the rain away. Water hits it, makes full contact, and rolls clean off without sticking. That's the posture I want toward hype, habits, and outcomes: fully engaged with the world, never glued to it.

My own day job sits right where AI meets infrastructure, so every week a genuine shift in computing collides with endless hype on my desk. The anchor I try to hold underneath any specific tool is older than this cycle: value hides in the gaps between layers. Most people look at systems as tidy stacks; I'm drawn to the dark seams between them, wiring things together until information actually flows. That doesn't expire when the next framework fades.

## Wrapping up

Seneca wrote that you shouldn't copy the crowd just because they're many, nor hate them just because they're different, which might be the best social-media guide from two thousand years ago. And Naval's line about desire ("a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want") hits both traps at once: chasing hype is desire, and fearing the trend is desire too. Both pull you out of the present.

Split that third line apart and each piece looks cheap: holding your ground looks like stubbornness, embracing change looks like chasing the wind, cherishing what you have sounds like a greeting card. Together, they're water on a lotus leaf.

Zeng Guofan again: a troubled mind and anxious thoughts are the forge that tempers you. Feeling pulled between hype and real trends isn't something to erase. Find the anchor underneath the tension, then step onto the field.

*Written on April 1, 2026, a morning when, predictably, three new AI frameworks dropped.*

今天早上我在本子上顺手写下三句话：

> 对热点，不要趋之若鹜。
> 对趋势，不要熟视无睹。
> 要固守本心，拥抱变化，珍惜所拥有的和遇到的一切。

写得很快。有些念头在心里滚了好几个月，自己都没察觉，到了点就自己冒了出来。

我做的是 AI 基础设施。每天睁眼打开电脑，都有一场新的“革命”在刷屏：vibe coding、又一个 agent 框架、又一家号称要掀翻一切的创业公司。上周还在满屏转发的 repo，这周已经没人提了。

可在这堆噪音底下，确实有根子上的东西在变。GPU 算力正在改写整个计算栈，我每天干的基础设施活儿，三年前几乎还不存在。对真变化装看不见，和追着每一个假风口跑，其实一样危险。

怎么把信号和噪音分开？没什么现成公式，但有三个锚点一直帮我稳住。

## 一、对热点，不要趋之若鹜

2019 年 1 月，WeWork 估值冲到 470 亿美元，九个月后连 IPO 都没上成，估值暴跌 78%，CEO 套现 7 亿美元走人，2023 年正式破产。2022 年 5 月，Terra/Luna 一周蒸发 450 亿美元，紧接着 FTX、Celsius、三箭资本像多米诺骨牌一样连片倒掉。冲进去的大多数人根本没搞懂底层逻辑，只是看见别人都在赚钱。

剧本翻来覆去都一样：一个真实的新东西（共享办公、区块链）被资本和 FOMO 一路吹上天，价格彻底脱钩价值，最后买单的永远是跟风的人。

巴菲特讲“能力圈”的那句话放到今天依然准：

> “圈的大小不重要，重要的是你知道边界在哪。”

追热点，就是人早就迈出了能力圈，心里还觉得自己站在里头。Naval 说得更干脆，用真实摆脱竞争。不追风口不等于躺平，而是逼着自己老实问一句：这件事我是真懂，还是纯粹怕错过？

AI 现在正跑在同一条轨道上。YC 合伙人 Jared Friedman 说过，2025 冬季批次里有四分之一公司 95% 的代码是 AI 写的。听着挺酷，可另一面同样扎眼：CodeRabbit 查了 470 个开源 PR，AI 参与写的那些安全问题是纯人工的 1.57 倍（XSS 漏洞高达 2.74 倍）；METR 2025 年初的随机实验里，16 位资深开源开发者在自己混熟的老仓库里干活，用上 AI 工具反而慢了 19%。连造出“vibe coding”一词的 Karpathy，给它的原定义都是“连代码的存在都忘掉”，听起来更像一句风险提示。同时 SEC 也开始重罚“AI washing”，给没多少真 AI 的产品贴个“AI-powered”标签，活脱脱是我们这个年代的 `.com` 后缀。

老子说上善若水，水善利万物而不争。水不往人堆里挤，专往别人嫌弃的低处流。不争不是不动，只是不被别人的节奏裹着走。

## 二、对趋势，不要熟视无睹

1975 年，柯达工程师 Steven Sasson 就造出了世界第一台数码相机，一万像素。拿给管理层看，人家算了算每年几十亿美元的胶卷生意，让他收起来。2012 年，柯达破产。他们不是没看懂技术，那技术本来就是自家发明的，只是看见了趋势，硬是扭开了头。

Blockbuster 的剧本更离谱。2000 年 Netflix 主动上门求收购，开价 5000 万美元，坐拥近 7700 家门店和 9.6 万名员工的 Blockbuster 笑着把人请了出去。十年后 Blockbuster 破产，Netflix 市值一路冲上几千亿美元。后来一位前高管承认，数字化确实会改写业务，但真正杀死 Blockbuster 的是它自己。

马可·奥勒留在《沉思录》里写过，“宇宙即变化，我们的生命由思想塑造”，还反复提醒自己，自然最爱干的事就是改变现有形态。这是一个罗马皇帝在军营和瘟疫里写下的实操笔记，不是鸡汤。老子同样拿水打比方：“天下莫柔弱于水，而攻坚强者莫之能胜。”水用不着硬碰硬变成石头，绕过去、渗进去，久而久之石头自己就裂了。

趋势就像水。你可以不喜欢，但没法假装它不存在。GPU 算力改写计算范式，是我每天上班面对的现实；LLM 重写软件工程的基本假设，也一样。

分辨热点和趋势，其实就看一个检验：钱一撤，它还在不在？

## 三、固守本心，拥抱变化，珍惜一切

前两句说起来容易，难在第三句：一边守住根基，一边跟着变化走，怎么同时做到？

NVIDIA 是最现成的答案。1993 年三个人在 Denny's 餐厅凑了每人 200 美元起家，1997 年账上只剩一个月现金，黄仁勋开会时的口头禅就是“我们离倒闭还有 30 天”。他后来说，当初要是知道这条路难一百万倍，可能压根不会干。

2006 年 NVIDIA 推出做通用 GPU 计算的 CUDA，游戏厂、科研圈、华尔街几乎没人搭理。手机芯片火的时候，黄仁勋也试过 Tegra，跑不出来就果断收手；他没蹭过社交热潮，也没在矿工买空显卡时把自己包装成加密货币公司，死死守了三十年 GPU 计算。等到 AI 浪潮真拍过来，全世界回头一看，只有 NVIDIA 手里握着全套底座。2025 年 10 月，它成了第一家市值跨过 5 万亿美元的公司。

《孙子兵法》讲“以正合，以奇胜”，后人提炼成四个字：守正出奇。守正是根基不动，出奇是在根基上灵活应变。NVIDIA 守的“正”是 GPU 计算，出的“奇”是每一波大浪打来时，它都已经站在岸边准备好了。

NBA 里也一样。马刺 1997 年选到邓肯后，波波维奇守了二十年的“正”：低位、传切、纪律。小球革命、抱团潮、三分时代轮番冲刷，别队忙着换教练换体系，马刺底盘不动，可到了 2014 年打出的 Beautiful Game 就是最漂亮的“奇”，三传两递把热火三巨头打得没脾气。库里 2015 年改写联盟看起来全是“奇”，底下压的也是从小练到大的投篮基本功和老库里那一代射手的底子。**所有的“奇”都长在“正”上；没有根基那不叫奇兵，叫散兵。**

曾国藩把话挑得更明：“凡天下事，在局外呐喊议论，总是无益；必须躬身入局，挺膺负责，方有成事之可冀。”守正出奇不是坐在场边想出来的，得先上场扛事。

佛教《法句经》第 401 偈里有个意象我一直记着：如同荷叶上的水珠，如同针尖上的芥子。荷叶从不拒绝雨水，水珠砸上来，跟叶子充分接触，却一点不沾，随后干净滑落。面对热点、现状和输赢，大概要的就是这个姿势：完全入局，但不被黏住。

我的工作刚好卡在 AI 和基础设施的交界口，每天桌上同时撞着真正的范式转移和满屏噪音。压在所有具体技术底下的，是我自己的一个老锚点：价值常藏在层与层的缝隙里。别人盯着整齐的技术栈，我更习惯往栈之间的暗处钻，把断点接通让信息流过去。这个习惯不会随着哪一轮热点过气，因为“织连接”本身不绑死在任何一层上。

## 最后几句

Seneca 说过：“不要因为他人众多就去模仿，也不要因为他人与你不同就去厌恶。”拿来当两千年前的社交媒体使用指南，刚好合适。Naval 那句“欲望是你和自己签的合同，拿到想要的之前你不许开心”也正好点破两头：追热点是欲望，怕错过趋势同样是欲望，两头都在把你从当下拽走。

回头看第三句话，拆开哪一块都容易走样：固守本心像死倔，拥抱变化像追风，珍惜一切又像鸡汤。合在一块，才是荷叶上的水珠，跟世界迎面相撞，又不被缠住。

曾国藩还写过：“困心衡虑，正是磨练英雄，玉汝于成。”在热点和趋势之间被来回拉扯，别急着嫌烦。把底下的锚点找准，然后上场干活就行。
