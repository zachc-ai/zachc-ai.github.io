Yuanming Hu started jotting down these notes when Meshy, the 3D AI company he founded, was at $80M in annual recurring revenue (by his own figures). He kept adding to the draft for about a month until the number crossed $100M, then spent five days on a business trip over China's National Day holiday cutting two-thirds of it. The 48 reflections that survived, grouped into five parts, went up on WeChat on October 7, 2026.

This is one I strongly agree with.

A year earlier he had reviewed Meshy's first sprint after product-market fit. This piece covers the "10 to 100" stretch around three questions: what he wishes someone had told him sooner, why he turned out the way he did and how a founder should treat a team, and where he still falls short.

## I. Warmth and principles

Hu credits his habit of putting others first to generous teachers: an informatics olympiad coach who always backed him, his MSRA mentor Steve Lin patiently fixing his papers, and his MIT co-advisors Fredo and Bill letting him spend his PhD on Taichi while writing the grant proposals themselves. His parents also raised him on the idea that taking a small loss can be a blessing. Putting others first looks costly up front, yet it builds deep trust and lets people do their best work, which is why empathy went straight into Meshy's culture code.

Still, a company isn't a school; it has to win to survive. Kindness without spine degrades into people-pleasing, and a pushover can't lead, can't keep principled teammates, and ends up driving out the strongest people. Kindness has to be judged across the whole team and over the long haul.

Fairness gets the most ink, and Hu calls it brutal systems engineering. Lazy managers always tax the dependable people: the quiet colleague who never complains gets handed the dirty work, crushes it, and gets rewarded with more dirty work. Without real compensation elsewhere, that punishes reliability. Paying loud talkers more than quiet builders, or rewarding long hours over output, is the same rot. His gut check before any raise or equity grant: if everyone's pay leaked tomorrow, would this still look fair?

Two more ground rules round out the section. First, candor: call bad work bad, soften the delivery without softening the facts, and judge people on whether they solved the problem. When someone isn't working out, parting ways today beats dragging it out for months. Second, weigh the whole board. Games hook us because feedback is instant and rules are black and white; management trades off short term against long term, individual against team, fairness against speed. A mine planted today might blow up a year later and take twice the effort to dig back out.

## II. Time and founder mode

The person at the top sets the ceiling on what a team understands, and time doesn't regenerate, so a founder's real judgment shows up in who they spend time with, on what, and in what order. That puts a ceiling on kindness too: drain yourself on trivia and you show up empty for the big calls.

Hu models a startup like a system of differential equations in time, with internal and external forces continuously feeding into each other. The job is separating leading indicators from lagging ones (he points to Ray Dalio's *The Changing World Order* for this lens). Revenue is only the visible tip; underneath sit talent density, clarity of vision, product and technical depth, culture, morale, shareholder alignment, and even the founder's health. Two companies posting identical numbers can be moving in opposite directions.

A Silicon Valley friend taught him how to price his own hour: take next year's target valuation minus today's, divide by 365 and by daily working hours to estimate the company's hourly opportunity cost, then assume half comes from the founders' calls. Once you see what a second of your time costs, a lot of minor gripes stop feeling worth agonizing over, and plenty of meetings drop off the calendar. Family and friends stay strictly outside that math.

On health, he observes that the best startup founders don't really have work-life balance. He borrows Jensen Huang's line about relaxing through work and prefers "work-life unification," while still cutting needless wear and tear: fewer spicy snacks and McDonald's spicy wings, less staying up late, and regular exercise.

Founders need ego, as long as it serves the mission rather than the urge to look right. You hold 100% of the context, your reports hold maybe half, and outsiders hold 1%, so outside criticism usually aims at an imaginary version of you. His programmer's metaphor: the CEO is the `try {...} catch {...}` inside `main`. Nobody sits above you to catch an exception, and if bad outside advice ends in `exit(-1)`, that crash belongs to you.

Founders are also lonely, and the danger is leaning on employees for emotional validation. The moment you need their applause, flattery gets rewarded, people start managing up, and you wake up inside an information bubble. Hu gets his emotional fuel elsewhere: promising himself a Hasselblad if the Series B closed (the photos in the post came from it, though he calls that lucky since most startups return zero), learning from stronger peers, and watching teammates who suffered alongside him grow and thrive.

He also separates two Chinese words: the opposite of 快乐 (joy) is pain, while the opposite of 幸福 (fulfillment) is boredom. The two are independent. He's lived through painful fulfillment as well as joyful fulfillment, and as long as he's growing, he's never bored.

## III. AI and people

Sometimes AI feels like an army at Hu's command; other times he feels like AI's "carbon-based pet," getting his curiosity spoon-fed tidy answers.

Without strong fundamentals, AI just magnifies muddled thinking. He's asked teammates to rewrite unreadable AI-generated reports by hand, poking fun at model tics like "not this, but that" twists, rhyming metaphors, and the mandatory thoughtful-sounding caveat tacked onto the end. Form and substance used to travel together; AI snapped that link and normalized confident nonsense.

He brings up the grading rule from Tsinghua's Yao class: right proof gets full marks, wrong proof gets zero, and writing "I don't know" gets half. That kills the trick of writing a wall of text hoping the grader misses the flaw. Meshy's version is simple: if you can't say it clearly, don't say it. Fuzzy writing usually hides fuzzy thinking.

Everyone at Meshy gets virtually unlimited tokens (an earlier post of his was titled "I Work for 10 Claude Codes"), yet interviews still test hand-written "old-method programming." Across hundreds of interviews, engineers who write clean logic by hand still solve hard problems better. Built on shaky fundamentals, AI output is a castle in the air. So in Hu's eyes, AI hasn't leveled the field; it has made the strong stronger and the weak weaker.

Still, AI has blown past his expectations in fields that close the loop in pure software (coding), have mechanical verification (math checked in Lean), and don't hinge on messy domain institutions like law. The anxiety follows him every day without costing him sleep. Earlier industrial revolutions built obedient tools; this time our pride and joy, intelligence itself, is being outrun in more and more arenas.

Math is his main exhibit. Once AI becomes the primary engine of proofs verified mechanically in Lean, mathematicians can know a theorem is true without understanding why. Referencing Scott Aaronson's "The Age of Wonders and Terrors" and Logan Graves's "Priest, Monk, and Mathematician," he notes the idea of mathematicians becoming priests interpreting AI's oracles, until AI interprets its own proofs better. On a smaller scale he already lives that way, asking AI to build interactive visualizations whenever he wants to learn something new.

Do humans still need to understand what AI writes? With coding agents, the practical cliff arrives the moment you no longer understand enough to write the next useful prompt. Production code still requires human comprehension today, though he won't guess for how long. Setting aside whether carbon-based life should step aside for silicon, he thinks AGI has effectively arrived in pockets, just unevenly distributed.

One neat upside closes the section: take tough problems to someone with no skin in the answer, since whoever diagnoses you might just be selling medicine. AI comes pretty close to having no agenda.

## IV. Team and management

A neutron weighs about 940 MeV/c², while the rest masses of its three quarks add up to around 12 MeV/c². Roughly 99% of the mass comes from the motion and strong-force interactions between quarks and gluons.

Teams work the same way. How people interact shapes output as much as raw individual talent, so Meshy hires for both fundamentals and culture fit. A handful of people can run on friendship; dozens need a leader who knows every detail of the business; hundreds need living principles and a deep bench.

Meshy pays at the top of the market, both out of goodwill and because high gross margins support it, though Hu is blunt that payroll can never outrun earnings. Echoing Zhang Yiming, he treats strong people as an investment rather than a cost, since in generative AI almost every breakout result comes from standout engineers.

Worse than being understaffed is over-hiring and diluting talent density, which he calls an irreversible organizational cancer. Strong people attract strong people; weak leaders hire weaker ones. His hiring rules are tight: every new hire should raise the bar, if you hesitate pass, and only hire self-driven people because nobody wants to micromanage.

Vision has to come from the founder's own gut: people building a ship should long for the sea, not just hammer nails, and a borrowed vision falls apart the first time a sharp teammate pushes on it. Nor should managers ever manipulate or neg reports, bosses, or peers (he recommends Robin Stern's book on gaslighting). Early on, a company has to extend its founder's instincts; as Jensen Huang put it, you're building an F1 car you're going to drive yourself, so build one you can actually drive.

Give the team room and don't kill ideas unless the logic is broken, using Andy Grove's task-relevant maturity (from *High Output Management*) to judge when to step back and when to get hands-on. Because AI startups are often run by young technical founders with little management mileage, Hu teaches an internal course called "Management for Good Students," having noticed that top individual performers often hit the hardest mental blocks when shifting into management.

He wants founder mode running across the whole leadership chain, with leaders diving two or three levels down and getting their hands dirty. Great engineers want to learn from their leaders; you can't judge work you've never touched; technical leaders who lose touch with details turn insecure and meddle; and real tradeoffs across talent, cash, brand, product, and market live in the details.

Some decisions resemble the office thermostat, where somebody is always freezing and somebody is sweating (he tells every new manager to play the first *Frostpunk*, not the second). Low performers also drive out high performers by lowering the bar together and blaming other teams after mistakes, so let mismatched people go decisively, and generously if margins allow.

Then he turns to 老登 (laodeng), Chinese internet slang for an older person who lectures and acts superior. Turning thirty this year, he wonders how younger readers see him. His counterexamples of aging without getting stale are Nintendo (137 years old), Hayao Miyazaki still directing at Studio Ghibli, and 77-year-old Ray Dalio. All of them keep players, audiences, and readers first. Shigeru Miyamoto used to pull a cleaner in from the hallway to test his game and watch silently over their shoulder (what Satoru Iwata called "Miyamoto-san's over-the-shoulder view"). In this era, failing to create fresh value is the real sin.

Laodeng behavior grows out of insecurity: fearing the world moved on and young people stopped listening. Every generation has its turn. The day Hu can no longer execute at the state of the art, he plans to hand the keyboard over and set quests instead, like *Zelda* dropping towers, shrines, Koroks, and Lynels across the map and letting players figure out the rest. (Nobody rescues Zelda that way, he jokes, which is fine as long as they're having fun.) Setting good quests for the sharpest young builders still requires learning fast; stop learning and laodeng creeps in at any age.

## V. Strategy and execution

Most effort in startups goes to waste, so picking what actually matters beats grinding harder. Take big bets and eat the losses cleanly. Calling his success luck isn't false modesty; he's simply used to failing. Real innovation fails most of the time, so he goes all in while expecting failure. Low expectations keep him sharp and happier, even if they tilt him a bit conservative.

Resources can cover for each other (talent density, culture, morale, brand, cash, vision, founder stamina), as long as no single weak spot falls below the floor.

From Professor Zeng Ming, he picked up working backward from the destination: instead of asking what you can build with today's hand, decide where you want to end up and figure out which cards you still need to collect. That lets you aim ten times bigger without necessarily working ten times harder. "Why can't I do this yet?" is table stakes; "What do I do today so I can later?" is the real question.

Tsinghua and MIT left him with two hard truths: the smartest people in the room often outwork you several times over, and the market only pays the best. Nobody wins casually, and anything luck hands you gets reclaimed by your real level over time. The people who reached the top also loved what they did; smart and hardworking loses to smart, hardworking, and in love with the craft. They were obsessive in experiments and checked every punctuation mark in papers. Hu used to read that fussiness as controlling until he saw it across Miyazaki, Miyamoto, Jobs, and his own writing mentors. Get priorities right first, then earn the right to obsess over details, accepting that high standards will earn pushback before the team sees the payoff.

The world is a giant makeshift operation, so simply nailing the basics puts you in the top 1%. The basics just happen to be brutal: know what you want, keep your word, sleep on time, think for yourself, write so readers can follow, ask sharp questions, use your own product daily, pay top contributors more, pick facts over face, and cut empty truisms. That means saying no relentlessly. As Meshy grows, Hu counts each "no" as a win: fewer projects leave room for strategy, fewer events leave time for family, fewer shallow acquaintances allow real friendships, and fewer shaky hires protect talent density.

His closing strategic point: don't panic about people copying your deep thinking. Real thinking fuses market timing, team strengths, founder judgment, and company DNA, so only you can run it at 100. A copycat tops out around 80, and the market takes the winner. If you can't be the best at something, skip it.

The post closes on a childhood dinner where his father and a former student who'd left to start a company remarked that a scientist treats science as home and an entrepreneur treats the company as home (both 科学家 and 企业家 end in 家, home). Twenty-plus years later Hu still carries that line. What science and business share, in his view, is simple: find a problem important and interesting enough to care about, then do the basic things properly.

胡渊鸣这份清单是从 Meshy 的 ARR 还在 8000 万美金时开始攒的。想到什么记什么，写了一个来月，按他文里的口径 ARR 跨过了一亿美金。国庆出差那五天，他删掉三分之二，剩下五个板块、48 条，10 月 7 日发在公众号上。

这篇我特别认。

去年他复盘过 Meshy 刚过 PMF、冲进高速增长时的得失。这一篇写的是“从 10 到 100”阶段，绕着三个问题转：进这个阶段前，最希望谁早点提醒自己什么；自己怎么长成现在这副脾气、founder 该怎么带团队；还有哪些地方没做好。

## 一、温度与原则

他“先人后己”的底色，来自一路碰上的好老师：中学打 OI 一直给他鼓劲的教练、在 MSRA 耐心逐字改 paper 的 Steve Lin，还有 MIT 两位 co-advisor Fredo 和 Bill，放手让他用整个博士阶段做 Taichi，写 grant proposal 这种苦差事也没往学生身上推。加上父母从小讲“吃亏是福”，他觉得先替别人想表面上像吃亏，换回来的却是极深的信任和团队放手干活的状态，所以 Meshy 一起步就把“同理心”写进了文化守则。

可公司不是学校，打不赢就活不下去。善良没了原则，就会滑成讨好型的滥好人：管不住团队，跟有原则的人尿不到一壶，最后把最厉害的人逼走。善，得放到整个团队和更长的时间尺子上量。

他花篇幅最多的是“公正”，管它叫极难的系统工程。管理者一偷懒，吃亏的一定是老实人：不声不响能扛事的同事接了一堆脏活，干得好，脏活就越派越多；别处不给补偿，等于明着惩罚责任心。会哭的孩子比闷头干活的拿得多、奖励加班不奖励产出，全是一个毛病。他给自己的检验很干脆：涨薪发期权前先想一下，要是哪天全员薪资意外泄露了，这个决定还站得住吗？

另外两条原则同样实在。一是客观真诚：干得不好就直说，语气可以留余地，事实不能糊弄，看人只看有没有真把问题解掉；真不合适就早分开，拖着对谁都不好。二是全局权衡：游戏让人上瘾，因为回报立等可取、规则非黑即白，现实管理却要把短期和长期、个人和团队、公平和效率全放在一杆秤上。今天顺手埋下去的雷，过几个月甚至几年才炸，挖出来得费双倍力气。

## 二、时间与 Founder mode

一号位的认知卡着团队的天花板，时间又没法再生，所以创始人真正的判断力，全落在跟谁待在一起、先干什么、按什么顺序干。这也给善良划了边界：把心力全耗在碎事上，轮到大决策时脑子空了，对团队照样不负责。

他把创业看成一组随时间 t 演化的常微分方程，内外部变量不停互相拽。一号位要分清 leading indicator 和 lagging indicator（他推荐 Ray Dalio 的《The Changing World Order》）。营收只是冰山露在水面上的尖，水面下是人才密度、愿景清不清、技术和产品硬不硬、文化、士气、股东齐不齐心，甚至创始人自己的身体。只盯营收和估值永远慢半拍，账面差不多的两家公司，可能一家正在起飞，另一家根子已经烂了。

硅谷一位朋友教他给自己的时间标价：明年目标估值减去今天估值，除以 365 再除以每天工时，算出全公司每小时的机会成本；假设一半来自创始团队决策，创始人每秒值多少钱一下就清楚了。数一算明白，好多破事根本不值当纠结，一堆会也不用开了。陪家人朋友除外，那块不进这套账。

聊到健康，他的观察挺直白：创业期最顶尖的 founder 几乎没有 WLB。他认同黄仁勋说自己干活就是在放松，更喜欢 work-life unification 这个说法，同时尽量少做无谓消耗：少吃辣条和麦辣鸡翅，少熬夜，挤出时间运动。

Founder 得有 ego，只是要服务于团队和愿景，不是为了证明自己嘴硬。你手里握着 100% 的上下文，下属大概一半，外人可能连 1% 都不到，所以外界批评打中的往往是他们想象出来的你。他用了个很程序员的比喻：CEO 就是 `main` 函数里那层 `try {...} catch {...}`，头顶上没人可以继续 raise exception，听信外行建议把公司带进沟里，`exit(-1)` 得自己兜着。

Founder 也很孤独，能聊到一块的大多只有别的 founder。坑在于忍不住向下属讨情绪价值：一旦渴求下属捧场，拍马屁就占了便宜，大家开始向上管理，创始人很快被包进信息茧房，甚至反过来被下属拿捏。他的情绪补给从别处来：跟自己约定 B 轮 close 就买台哈苏（文章里的照片就是它拍的，他也承认这很幸运，多数创业最后归零）、跟更强的人共事偷师，还有看着一路扛过来的同事越过越好。

他还分了“快乐”和“幸福”：快乐的反面是痛苦，对他来说幸福的反面是无聊，两者互不冲突。这两年他吃过快乐的幸福，也吃过痛苦的幸福（他叫“酸爽”），只要还在长进，就不会无聊。

## 三、AI 与人

有时 AI 像他手底下的千军万马，有时他倒像 AI 养的“碳基小宠物”，好奇心被整理好的知识一口口喂着。

基本功不行，AI 只会把糊涂放大。他直接让同事把看不懂的 AI 报告重写，也吐槽模型最爱用的“不是……而是……”绕弯句、押韵却空洞的比喻，还有结尾硬加一句装深刻的 “One honest caveat”。以前形式和内容多少绑在一起，AI 把这条线扯断了，一本正经地胡说八道成了日常。

他想起姚班的判分规矩：证明题答对满分，答错零分，老实写“我不会”给一半。写一大篇长文蒙混过关的路子直接废了。搬到 Meshy 就是说不清就别说，表达绕来绕去，多半是脑子里本身就没想透。

Meshy 全员几乎无限量用 token（他之前写过《我给 10 个 Claude Code 打工》），面试却照样考手写代码的“古法编程”。几百场面试看下来，能徒手写出清晰逻辑的人，解难题的能力依然更强。地基虚，AI 往上砌得再高也是空中楼阁。所以 AI 没把门槛拉平，反倒让强者更强、弱者更弱。

不过他也承认，有几类领域 AI 的进步已经甩开想象：纯软件里能闭环、不用搬原子（比如写代码），结果能机械验证（比如用 Lean 验数学证明），又不需要法律那种复杂制度知识。这让他每天都带着对人类未来的焦虑，倒还不至于失眠。从前几次工业革命造出来的都是听话的工具，这次被反超的，是人类最引以为傲的智力本身。

数学是最明显的例子。等 AI 成为定理证明主力、Lean 负责机械验真，数学家可能只知道结论绝对对，却既看不懂也学不到东西。他推荐了 Scott Aaronson 的《The Age of Wonders and Terrors》和 Logan Graves 的《Priest, Monk, and Mathematician》，提到数学家可能退化成解读 AI 神谕的“祭司”，再往后连解读也会交给 AI。在小尺度上他自己已经这么过了：碰到不懂的概念，直接让 AI 写个交互式可视化帮自己学。

那人还要不要看懂 AI 在干嘛？在 coding agent 里，真看不懂的代价是你写不出下一条能把问题往前推的 prompt。至少眼下写生产代码还得懂，至于能撑多久不好说。先把碳基要不要让位给硅基放一边，他觉得某种意义上 AGI 已经到了，只是在不同领域分布极不均匀。

这一节结尾留了条挺巧的用法：碰上棘手事，找个跟你没利益牵扯的人聊。说你有病的人，没准正想卖药给你。这么算下来，AI 至少基本做到了没私心。

## 四、团队与管理

一颗中子重约 940 MeV/c²，里面三个夸克的静质量加起来才 12 MeV/c² 左右，剩下约 99% 全来自强相互作用下夸克和胶子的运动和场能。

团队也一样，人和人怎么互动，跟单兵能力同样决定产出，所以 Meshy 招人既盯基本功也盯 culture fit。几个人靠兄弟情能转，几十个人靠一号位吃透一线细节，上百人就得靠真落地的原则和够厚的人才梯队。

Meshy 给到市场最高一档薪资，一半是善意，一半靠高毛利撑腰，他也直言薪酬绝不能跑在公司赚钱能力前面。他赞同张一鸣的话，把人叫“人力投资”而不是“人力成本”，因为在生成式 AI 里，成绩几乎全靠最优秀的那批人打出来。

比缺人可怕得多的是乱扩招把人才密度冲稀，他称之为不可逆的组织癌症。牛人吸牛人，弱 leader 只敢招更弱的。所以他的招人底线极硬：每个新人都要比现有团队强；让你犹豫的人干脆不招，后面八成出事；只招自驱的人，没人愿意天天盯梢做微观管理。

愿景必须由创始人自己想透。造船的人先得向往大海，不然每天只是在砸钉子；抄来的愿景经不起团队追问，连自己这关都过不了。前面说 founder 要防被下属拿捏，反过来管理者也别去打压或贬低（negging）下属、老板和平级同事（他推荐了 Robin Stern 写煤气灯效应的书）。早期公司得是创始人的延伸，就像黄仁勋说的，你在造一辆自己要开的 F1，就得造一辆自己真开得顺手的。

给团队留空间，逻辑没硬伤就别一上来毙掉；什么时候放手、什么时候抓细，按 Andy Grove《High Output Management》里的 task-relevant maturity 来拿捏。AI 公司多由年轻技术人掌舵，普遍缺管理经验，他在内部专门开了门《给好学生的管理课》，因为发现单兵越强的好学生，转管理时的心理坎往往越高。

他希望 founder mode 跑遍整个管理层，leader 得能往下看两三层、必要时亲自动手。厉害的人想跟着 leader 长本事；自己没干过就判不了下属好坏；不懂细节的技术 leader 容易心里发虚、怕被替代，反倒乱插手；人才、现金、品牌、产品和市场的全局取舍，也全藏在细节里。

有些决定就像办公室空调温度，总有人嫌热有人嫌冷，新管理者都该去玩玩《冰汽时代》（记得玩一代）。表现差的人还会逼走表现好的人：干砸了互相安慰、一起拉低标准、把锅甩给别的部门。想留住强人，就果断跟不合适的人分开，毛利够的话尽量好聚好散。

接着他聊到“老登”。今年刚满三十，他也琢磨自己在更年轻的读者眼里算不算“中登”。他举的“老而不登”的标杆是 137 岁的任天堂、宫崎骏还在画片的吉卜力，还有 77 岁的 Ray Dalio，共同点是永远把玩家、观众和读者摆第一位。宫本茂会从走廊拽个清洁工进来试玩游戏，自己站背后一声不吭看反应，岩田聪管这叫“宫本先生的越肩视线”。这个时代，不再创造新价值才是原罪。

登味的根子是不安全感：怕被时代甩下、怕年轻人不听自己的。其实一代人有一代人的仗，被甩下很正常。哪天自己干不到 state of the art 了，就交出去，自己退一步设目标，像塞尔达那样把鸟望台、神庙、呀哈哈和莱尼尔摆好，让玩家自己去解。（他自嘲这样就没人救公主了，不过玩家玩开心就行。）难的是，想给最聪明的年轻人出好题，自己得一直跟得上；一旦停止快速学习，不管几岁，登味都会找上门。

## 五、战略与执行

创业里绝大多数忙活都是白费，花时间想清什么真正重要，比埋头猛干值钱得多。要敢下大注，输了干脆认账。他把成功归给运气不是谦虚，而是太习惯失败了：搞创新本来就是大概率失败，那就抱着可能失败的心态全力以赴。低预期让人保持警醒也更容易开心，代价只是略微保守。

各种资源可以互相补位（人才密度、文化、士气、品牌、现金、愿景、founder 的体力），只要哪块别掉穿底线，都能拿别的长板顶上。

从曾鸣教授那儿，他学了“以终为始”：别盯着手里现有几张牌盘算能干嘛，先想将来要做成什么，再倒推去补牌。这样敢做大十倍的事，还不见得更难。“我今天为什么做不到”是基本功，更好的问法是“我今天做什么，将来才做得到”。

清华和 MIT 给过他两记很实在的提醒：身边最聪明的人往往还比你拼几倍，而且市场只认第一名。没人能随随便便成事，靠运气撞上的红利，拉长看都会按真实实力吐回去。那里最后跑出来的人还真爱干这行，光聪明加努力，干不过聪明、努力又热爱的人。他们做实验死磕，写 paper 连标点都要抠对。他早年觉得这种较真有点控制欲，后来发现在宫崎骏、宫本茂、乔布斯，还有带他写英文 paper 的老师身上全都有。前提是先把大优先级排对，把基本盘做稳再吹毛求疵，同时认了提出超前高要求时眼下肯定要挨骂。

世界本来就是个巨大的草台班子，把最基本的几件事做扎实就能进前 1%，只是基本功恰恰最难：想清自己要什么、说到做到、按时睡觉、独立思考、确保读者看得懂自己写的东西、只问好问题、每天用自家产品、让贡献大的人多拿、事实压过面子、不说正确的废话。正因为难，就得狠心做减法。公司越大，说“不”越值钱：少开项目才挤得出时间想战略，少跑活动才能陪家人，少泛泛交朋友才能深交同路人，少招凑数的人才能保住人才密度。

最后一条，深入的思考被别人抄去也没什么好怕的。它长在市场窗口、团队实力、创始人判断和公司基因上，只有你自己能跑到 100 分，抄作业的顶多跑出 80 分，而市场只挑最好的。做不到最好，不如干脆不做。

结尾是他小时候的一顿饭。父亲带着一位出去创业的旧学生吃饭，两人聊到：科学家把科学当家，企业家把企业当家。二十多年过去他一直记着。搞科学和做企业到底哪里相通？他的答案很朴素：找一个足够重要又足够好玩的问题，然后把最基础的事扎扎实实做好。
