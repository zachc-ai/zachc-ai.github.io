Kevin Kelly first published *1,000 True Fans* in 2008. The version on his site now is a later rewrite, the one that also went into Tim Ferriss's *Tools of Titans*. The longer original sits below it on the same page. I read both and took notes on the core argument.

## You don't need millions

His claim is simple. To make a living as a creator, you don't need millions of customers, clients, or fans. A few thousand true fans can be enough, if you are content to make a living and not a fortune.

A true fan, in his definition, will buy anything you produce. They will drive 200 miles to hear you sing, buy the hardcover, paperback, and audiobook, pay for a "best-of" of a free YouTube channel, or come to your chef's table once a month. He also calls them super fans.

## Two criteria

The math needs two conditions.

First, you have to make enough each year to earn, on average, $100 in profit from each true fan. That is easier in some kinds of work than others. He treats it as a useful challenge anyway, because it is usually easier to give more to people who already care than to find new ones.

Second, the relationship has to be direct. They pay you, and you keep the money, instead of taking a thin cut from a label, publisher, studio, or retailer. Keep the full $100, and 1,000 true fans come to $100,000 a year. That, he says, is a living for most people.

A thousand paying people is a much more realistic target than a million, especially at the start. You might even remember a thousand names. Add one true fan a day, and it only takes a few years.

## 1,000 is not a magic number

He is clear that 1,000 is an order of magnitude, three orders below a million, not a fixed quota. If you only earn $50 per true fan per year, you need 2,000 of them. If you can earn $200, 500 may be enough. If you only need $75,000 a year to live on, you can aim lower. A duo, or anyone with a partner, should roughly double the number. If the team grows by 33%, the fan base only needs to grow by 33% too.

Another way he puts it: can you please someone enough to earn one day's wages from them each year? That is a high bar, but not an impossible one for 1,000 people worldwide.

## True fans and the people around them

Not every fan is a super fan. For each true fan, he says, you might have two or three regular ones. Picture concentric circles, with true fans in the middle and regular fans around them. The outer circle buys only sometimes, or only once, and might add about 50% more income. You still focus on the true fans. They are the direct source of income, and also the people most likely to bring the others in.

In the 2008 essay he called that outer circle Lesser Fans. One way he framed the work back then was converting a thousand Lesser Fans into a thousand True Fans.

## Why a small niche can still work

Direct relationships are an old default. What changed, he says, is the last century of retail: most creators, and often even their publishers and labels, never knew the names of their dedicated customers. More middlemen meant you needed a much larger audience to get by.

The web gave anyone payment and communication tools to sell to someone on the other side of the world, and to keep the full price. That alone lowers the number of fans you need.

He adds a second point about reach. On a network like the web, the most obscure book or song is only one click from the most popular one. Large aggregators noticed that the slow-selling items, taken together, could rival the hits. His successor at Wired, Chris Anderson, called this the long tail. Recommendation systems and search made the obscure a little less obscure.

The example he uses is a small town. There are about 2 million of them on Earth, and you might be the only person in yours who wants some very specific thing. Online, your 1,000 true fans are one click away. He puts the bar low: almost anything can interest at least one person in a million. Even at that rate, with a world of about 7 billion people, you would have about 7,000 potential fans. So a one-in-a-million taste can still find 1,000 true fans. The hard part is being found.

Big companies, in his view, are poorly built for this kind of niche. That leaves the long tail more open to individual creators. Social media, he says, has only made gathering and keeping those fans easier.

## Crowdfunding

The rewrite adds crowdfunding. Letting fans fund the next thing you make for them is, in his words, a good deal on both sides. He writes that there were about 2,000 crowdfunding platforms worldwide. Some fund a finished project, some require an all-or-nothing goal, and some, like Patreon, support ongoing work: a monthly magazine, a video series, or a salary.

The largest one he names is Kickstarter. At the time of the rewrite, he says it had raised $2.5 billion for more than 100,000 projects, and a successful campaign had 241 backers on average, far fewer than a thousand. If you already have 1,000 true fans, you can run a campaign, because a true fan will fund it by definition. Whether it works still depends on what you ask of them.

He also notes that this path is not a binary choice. He has used New York publishers, self-publishing, and Kickstarter, depending on the work. In every case, he says, having true fans helps the route you pick.

## It isn't for everyone

Cultivating a thousand true fans takes time. Done well, it can become another full-time job, or at least a demanding part-time one. Some creators do not want to deal with fans, and he thinks some honestly should not. They can hire someone to do it, though that helper raises the number of fans you need, or they can leave the job to a label or publisher. Those middle people, he warns, are often even worse at it than you would be.

Stardom can still be a goal. What he wants to leave you with is another path: a direct tie to people who actually care, instead of a chase for a hit. You may never reach a thousand. Even then, he thinks this is a saner place to aim, and a more reachable one.

## A short note

I'm not sure the $100 figure still fits every kind of work. The second condition feels more durable: they pay you directly. Making things keeps getting cheaper, and AI is part of that. The part that does not get cheaper on its own is the relationship.

Kevin Kelly 的《1,000 True Fans》最早发表于 2008 年。他后来改过一版，收进了 Tim Ferriss 的《Tools of Titans》。现在他网站上放的，就是这版改写，更长的原文附在同一页后面。两版我都读了，记下这篇的核心意思。

## 不必追一百万

他的判断很简单。要靠创作过日子，你不需要一百万客户，也不需要一百万粉丝。只要大约几千个 true fans，就有可能够用。前提是，你满足于过日子，而不是发财。

按他的定义，true fan 会买你出的所有东西。他们愿意开 200 英里的车去听你唱歌，精装、平装、有声书都会买，下一件周边没见过也下单，免费 YouTube 频道出了精选，他们也愿意付钱，或者每个月来一次你的 chef's table。他也把这种人叫做 super fans。

## 两个条件

这套算法要成立，得同时满足两件事。

第一，你每年做出来的东西，平均能从每个 true fan 身上留下 100 美元利润。有的行当容易一些，有的难一些。他觉得这仍然是个值得接的挑战，因为给已经在意你的人更多，通常比再去找新人容易。

第二，关系必须是直接的。钱付给你，也留在你这儿，而不是经过厂牌、出版社、工作室或零售商，只拿到一小截。100 美元都能留下，一千个 true fans 就是一年 10 万美元。他说，对大多数人，这已经够过日子了。

一千个肯付钱的人，比一百万粉丝好瞄准得多，尤其是刚起步的时候。你甚至可能记得住一千个名字。一天增加一个 true fan，只要几年就够了。

## 一千不是死数字

他也说清楚了，1000 看的是数量级，比一百万少了三个零，不是固定配额。如果每个 true fan 一年只能带来 50 美元，你就需要 2000 人。如果能到 200 美元，500 人也许就够。如果你一年有 7.5 万美元就过得去，目标还可以再往下调。两个人搭档，或者有合伙人，人数大概要翻一倍。团队扩大 33%，需要的粉丝也只需多 33%。

还有一种算法：你能不能让一个人高兴到，愿意把一年里一天的薪水给你？这个门槛不低，但在全世界找一千个这样的人，还是能找着的。

## 中心是 true fans

当然，不是每个粉丝都是 super fan。他估计，每一个 true fan 周围，可能还有两三个普通粉丝。可以想成一组同心圆，true fans 在中间，普通粉丝围在外面。外圈的人只是偶尔买，或者只买过一次，也许能再带来大约 50% 的收入。但力气还是该放在 true fans 身上。他们既是直接的收入来源，也最可能把外圈的人带进来。

2008 年那一版里，他把外圈叫做 Lesser Fans。当时他还有一种说法：你要做的事，就是把一千个 Lesser Fans，变成一千个 True Fans。

## 为什么小众也做得起来

跟顾客直接打交道，本来是更老的做法。他觉得真正变了的，是过去一百年的零售：大多数 creator，甚至连出版社和厂牌，往往都不知道核心顾客叫什么名字。中间商越多，你就越需要更大的受众，才能过下去。

后来有了网络上的支付和沟通工具，任何人都可以把作品卖给地球另一边的人，并且留下全款。只这一条，需要的粉丝数就会下来。

他还补了第二点，关于能不能被找到。在网络这样的点对点系统里，最冷门的书或歌，离最热门的也只有一次点击。大型平台后来发现，卖得慢的那些东西加在一起，总量可以跟畅销品相当。他在 Wired 的继任者 Chris Anderson 把这叫做长尾。推荐系统和搜索，让冷门的东西不那么看不见了。

他举的例子是小镇。地球上大约有 200 万个小镇，你在自己镇上，可能是唯一一个想要某件非常具体的东西的人。到了网上，你的一千个 true fans 离你只有一次点击。他把门槛放得很低：几乎任何东西，都能让一百万人里至少一个人感兴趣。就算真的只有百万分之一这么少，按当时全球大约 70 亿人算，也大约有 7000 个潜在粉丝。也就是说，只有“百万分之一”这么小众的口味，仍然找得到一千个 true fans。难的是让他们找到你。

在他看来，大公司并不擅长对接这种小众受众。长尾这块空档，反而更多地留给了单个 creator。他说，社交媒体只是让聚集和留住这些人变得更容易了。

## crowdfunding

改写版里加了 crowdfunding。让粉丝为你下一步要做给他们的东西出资，他说这对两边都划算。他写到，当时全世界大约有 2000 个 crowdfunding 平台。有的为已经完成的项目筹资，有的必须达到目标才算成功，有的则像 Patreon，为还在进行的工作提供持续支持，比如月刊、视频系列，或者一份薪水。

他点名最大的是 Kickstarter。按改写时的数字，它已经为超过 10 万个项目筹集了 25 亿美元，成功项目的平均支持者是 241 人，远不到一千。如果你已经有一千个 true fans，就有条件去做这样的筹款，因为按定义，true fan 会参与资助。能不能成，仍然取决于你向他们要什么。

他也说，这条路不是只能选一个、把别的都关掉。他自己用过纽约的大出版社，也自己出过书，还用 Kickstarter 出给 true fans，看内容和目的而定。无论走哪条路，养着这批 true fans，都有帮助。

## 并不适合所有人

养一千个 true fans 很花时间。做得认真，它可能变成另一份全职工作，至少也是一份很沉的兼职。有些人不想自己对付粉丝，他也觉得有些人确实不该自己来。可以找人代劳，不过帮手会提高你需要的粉丝数；也可以把这件事交给厂牌或出版社。他提醒，这些中间方往往比你自己做得更差。

他也没有把成名说成坏事。他只是指出还有另一条路：跟真正在意你的人直接连上，不必去追一次爆款。你也许永远凑不满一千人。就算这样，他也觉得这个方向更清醒，也更可能走到。

## 顺便记一句

100 美元这个数字，放到现在未必还适合每一种工作，我不太确定。更经得住时间的，是第二个条件：钱要直接付给你。做东西的成本还在往下走，AI 也在加快这件事。跟愿意付钱的人处好关系，这件事不会自己变便宜。
